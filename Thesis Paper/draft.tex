\begin{abstract}
Connected vehicles must keep heterogeneous Electronic Control Units (ECUs) current
via Over-the-Air (OTA) firmware updates, yet coordinating updates across engine,
braking, and infotainment ECUs under a shared safety-bounded memory budget and
stochastic wireless channel is a hard scheduling problem. We formulate this as
cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) in which each ECU is an
independent agent, supervised by a Control-Barrier-Function safety shield and a
Monte Carlo Shapley credit-assignment scheme. We then ask: does a heterogeneous
fleet require an explicitly specialized policy per ECU type, as FP3O~\cite{b2}
provides, or does a single shared policy conditioned on ECU type suffice? Using a
staged design that progressively increases functional conflict between ECU types, we
uncover and correct a critical observation-design confound: a raw per-agent numeric
identifier that silently converted the shared policy into a hidden per-agent lookup
table, artificially inflating its advantage. After correcting the confound and scaling
to 5 seeds (2,000,897 timesteps each), type-conditioned IPPO and MAPPO are both
statistically indistinguishable and dramatically more stable (mean return \textbf{16.71},
95\,\% CI $\pm0.001$ each) than the explicitly specialized FP3O architecture (mean
return \textbf{5.09}, 95\,\% CI $\pm26.14$), whose variance is dominated by one
catastrophic seed ($-32.37$). The safety shield holds fleet memory-safety violations
at 0\,\% for all algorithms. Our results extend the ``surprising effectiveness of
PPO'' \cite{b3} to a vehicular cyber-physical setting with hard safety constraints:
for heterogeneous fleet OTA schedulers, all you need is type conditioning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs) are distributed computing platforms
built from dozens of Electronic Control Units (ECUs) governing engine timing, braking,
infotainment, and driver-assistance functions. Like any networked software system,
these ECUs require periodic firmware updates to patch vulnerabilities and add features,
and Over-the-Air delivery has become the industry standard for avoiding costly recalls
and reducing update latency across large fleets.

Coordinating updates across a heterogeneous ECU fleet is non-trivial. A braking ECU
and an infotainment unit have different risk profiles, memory budgets, and, once
per-operation cost structure is introduced, structurally different optimal update
strategies. Updates are delivered over a shared, stochastic wireless channel, so the
scheduler must decide which firmware blocks to send first, using which update technique,
to which ECU, subject to a fleet-wide memory-safety budget that must not be exceeded.

ReLES-OTA~\cite{b1} demonstrated that single-agent deep RL can solve this problem more
efficiently than heuristic baselines, but its single-agent formulation implicitly treats
the entire vehicle as one decision point. That abstraction becomes unrealistic as ECU
diversity grows. We reframe fleet-wide OTA scheduling as a cooperative MARL problem
where each ECU is its own agent, and we investigate a design question of direct
engineering relevance: \emph{does a heterogeneous ECU fleet need an explicitly
specialized policy per type, as FP3O~\cite{b2} provides, or does a single shared
policy conditioned on a semantic ECU-type vector suffice?}

Architectures such as FP3O~\cite{b2} resolve per-type incentive conflicts through
dedicated action heads per agent class. Yu et al.~\cite{b3} argue that fully shared
IPPO/MAPPO policies are competitive in cooperative settings because they pool all
agents' experience, though this finding has mostly been validated on homogeneous
benchmarks. LeRoy and Kolb~\cite{b21} showed that IPPO also generalizes to novel
teammate algorithms in a heterogeneous robotics task without explicit partner diversity
during training. Our paper provides the vehicular cyber-physical counterpart, under
hard safety constraints and conflicting per-type cost incentives.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A safety-constrained, Shapley-credited multi-agent OTA scheduling environment
for heterogeneous CAV fleets, extending~\cite{b1} to the cooperative MARL setting.
\item A staged, confound-aware empirical comparison of specialized (FP3O) against
type-conditioned shared (IPPO, MAPPO) policies under genuine ECU-type conflict.
\item Identification and correction of an agent-identity observation confound that
silently converts any nominally shared policy into a per-agent lookup table,
confirming the caution in~\cite{b14} with a concrete numerical instance.
\item Evidence, across 5 seeds, that type-conditioned sharing matches or exceeds
FP3O's mean return while being substantially more stable across random initializations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{OTA update scheduling.} ReLES-OTA~\cite{b1} treats single-vehicle OTA
scheduling as a codec-construction MDP solved with single-agent PPO, reducing payload
and memory overhead versus heuristic baselines. We extend this to multi-agent.

\textbf{Cooperative MARL.} CTDE methods include value-decomposition approaches such
as VDN~\cite{b12} and QMIX~\cite{b13}, and actor-critic methods such as
MADDPG~\cite{b10} and COMA~\cite{b11}. Yu et al.~\cite{b3} demonstrated that fully
shared IPPO/MAPPO are competitive with more complex MARL architectures through full
experience pooling. FP3O~\cite{b2} shares a backbone but routes it into per-type
specialized heads for heterogeneous fleets. Terry et al.~\cite{b14} caution that
parameter-sharing benefits depend critically on how agent identity is encoded in the
observation space, a point this paper validates numerically. LeRoy and Kolb~\cite{b21}
reported a parallel result in cooperative robotics: IPPO generalizes to novel teammate
policies in heterogeneous teams without partner-diverse training.

\textbf{Safety-constrained vehicular MARL.} We adopt a rule-based CBF-inspired
safety filter~\cite{b15} that provides hard guarantees regardless of policy quality.
RSR-RSMARL~\cite{b16} and MAPPO-PIS~\cite{b17} apply CBF-based shields to
multi-agent driving; we apply the same design philosophy to OTA memory-budget enforcement.

\textbf{Credit assignment.} We use a Monte Carlo Shapley approximation~\cite{b18} to
attribute shared team reward to individual agents once ECU types carry different
operation costs, avoiding the bias of a naive equal-split.

\section{Environment and Methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
We model the fleet as a cooperative partially observable decision process. At time
$t$, ECU agent $i$ observes a local state $o_i^t$ containing its firmware backlog,
available operations, memory state, channel-related quantities, and a semantic type
vector $t_i$. Each agent selects an action $a_i^t$ from the masked set of valid
firmware-block/operation pairs. The joint action is
$\mathbf{a}^t=(a_1^t,\ldots,a_N^t)$, and the environment returns a shared fleet
objective together with per-agent credit assignments. For type-conditioned IPPO, the
actor can be written as
\[
a_i^t \sim \pi_\theta(\,\cdot \mid o_i^t,t_i),
\]
where the same parameters $\theta$ are used by every agent. FP3O instead introduces
type-specific policy components on top of a shared representation. The central
question is therefore whether the semantic variable $t_i$ provides enough
information for one shared policy to represent different role-dependent actions
without explicit parameter specialization.

The policy seeks to maximize the expected discounted return
\[
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right],
\]
where the reward reflects the modeled update cost, stochastic transmission cost, and
memory overhead, while the Shapley-based allocation assigns the resulting team
contribution to individual agents. The safety shield separately enforces the
modeled fleet-memory constraint. This separation lets us study the architectural
question without conflating cooperative optimization with constraint enforcement.

\subsection{Observation, Action, and Reward Design}
Each agent's observation contains its local firmware state and the variables needed
to estimate the consequence of a candidate update operation. The action space is the
choice of a firmware block and one of the supported operations (Copy, Modify, or
Multi-Base), with invalid block/operation combinations masked before policy
sampling. The environment's reward combines the modeled encoding/transmission cost
and memory overhead. Type-specific operation multipliers modify these costs in S3/S4,
so the same operation can be preferable for one ECU type and undesirable for another.
This creates a controlled setting in which explicit specialization has a plausible
advantage, making a competitive shared policy a meaningful result rather than a
consequence of identical per-type objectives.

The reward should therefore be interpreted as a simulated scheduling objective rather
than a direct measure of road safety. Safety is handled separately by the action
shield, which prevents the policy from violating the modeled fleet-memory budget
during exploration.

\subsection{Single-Agent Baseline}
We reproduce the ReLES-OTA~\cite{b1} cost model in a single-agent
Gymnasium~\cite{b8} environment. At each step an agent selects a firmware block and an
update operation (Copy, Modify, or Multi-Base) and receives a reward combining encoding
cost, a stochastic truncated-Gaussian transmission cost, and memory overhead. PPO
reaches a mean return of approximately $-18$, consistent with the original result,
validating the cost model before scaling to multi-agent.

\subsection{Multi-Agent Environment}
We extend to a PettingZoo Parallel-API~\cite{b9} setting with $N=4$ ECU agents, each
maintaining an independent 16-block firmware backlog with a per-step action mask.
Three components address safety and fairness in fleet scheduling.

\textit{Death masking}: agents whose backlogs are cleared before the episode ends
receive a fixed zero-vector observation that preserves only their type identity, keeping
tensor shapes constant for the centralized critic following the convention in~\cite{b17}.

\textit{Safety shield}: a rule-based filter intercepts every proposed joint action and
blocks any action that would push cumulative fleet memory above 85\,\% of budget,
guaranteeing safety independent of policy quality during high-variance early exploration.


Here, ``safety'' refers specifically to the fleet-memory constraint represented by
the simulator. The shield therefore provides a hard guarantee with respect to that
modeled constraint, rather than a guarantee of physical vehicle safety in a real
vehicle.

\textit{Monte Carlo Shapley credit}: each agent's per-step reward is its approximate
Shapley value~\cite{b18}, computed by averaging marginal contributions across random
coalition orderings, to attribute individual contribution fairly under heterogeneous
per-operation costs.

\subsection{Policy Architectures}
All architectures follow the CTDE paradigm. \textbf{IPPO} trains one shared MLP across
all agents. \textbf{MAPPO} shares the actor but pairs it with a centralized critic
conditioned on the joint observation. \textbf{FP3O}~\cite{b2} uses a shared MLP
backbone (256$\to$256$\to$128) that routes into per-ECU-type specialized action and
value heads. FP3O operationalizes the hypothesis that explicit per-type specialization
is necessary; IPPO and MAPPO test whether type-conditioned parameter sharing suffices.

\subsection{Type Conditioning vs.\ Agent-Identity Conditioning}
\label{sec:type-cond}
A key distinction underlies this work. A per-agent \emph{index} included in the
observation is \emph{identifying}: it lets a shared network memorize distinct
sub-policies per physical agent, collapsing the difference between shared and
specialized architectures while retaining the sample-efficiency advantage of shared
weights. A per-type \emph{semantic vector} is \emph{non-identifying}: two agents of
the same type receive identical vectors and the network must learn a single policy for
that role. Type conditioning encodes functional role rather than physical identity.
A policy trained on type vectors generalizes to fleets of arbitrary size or composition,
provided the type vocabulary is fixed; one trained on agent indices does not. The
type-conditioned setup is therefore the methodologically correct test of whether a
single shared network suffices for role-heterogeneous fleets.

\subsection{Training Stability}
At full scale (2M timesteps), extreme PPO probability ratios combined with rare large
crash penalties from the safety shield caused Adam's~\cite{b19} second-moment estimates
to overflow, producing NaN logits. The following measures restored stability: (i) logit
clamp to $[-10,10]$; (ii) target-KL early-stopping; (iii) replacing a custom value
normalizer with \texttt{VecNormalize}~\cite{b20}; (iv) capping the maximum crash
penalty. All subsequent runs converged stably across seeds.

\subsection{Staged Heterogeneity Design}
ECU-type heterogeneity is introduced in four stages to attribute each performance
gap to a specific cause.
\textbf{S1} (homogeneous): type labels carry no reward effect.
\textbf{S2} (uniform multiplier): a per-type scalar is applied to all operation costs.
\textbf{S3} (operation-specific multiplier): safety-critical types are incentivized
toward Multi-Base updates; infotainment toward Copy, creating genuine policy conflict,
but with the agent-identity confound still present in observations.
\textbf{S4} (corrected): identical to S3, with the confound removed and experiments
scaled to 5 seeds.

\section{Experimental Setup}
All headline experiments use $N=4$ ECU agents, 16 firmware blocks per agent, and
2,000,897 timesteps per seed, with the safety-shield threshold at 85\,\% of the memory
budget. The S4 comparison uses 5 seeds per algorithm (15 independent runs total;
approximately 10M environment steps per algorithm). The operation-specific cost
multipliers that create genuine type conflict are shown in Table~\ref{tab:multipliers}.
Engine and braking are structurally incentivized toward Multi-Base updates; infotainment
toward Copy. This ordinal conflict cannot be resolved by a single operation-agnostic
policy and is the setting in which specialization should provide its greatest advantage.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
All headline S4 comparisons use the same environment, training horizon, safety
constraint, and evaluation procedure. Five independent random seeds are used for
each algorithm, and the individual seed results are retained rather than reporting
only an aggregate mean. The reported confidence interval is the 95\,\% interval
computed across seed-level returns. Because $n=5$ is small, the inferential
statistics are treated as directional evidence rather than definitive evidence of
superiority. In particular, we do not describe a comparison as statistically
significant when its reported Welch test does not reach $p<0.05$.

The FP3O collapsed seed is retained in all aggregates rather than being removed as
an outlier. This is important because the run-to-run instability is itself an
observation of the experiment. All algorithms are evaluated under the same
zero-violation safety constraint, so return differences should not be interpreted
as differences in safety violations.

\section{Results}

\textbf{S1 -- Homogeneous fleet.} IPPO and MAPPO outperform FP3O (approximately
12.85 vs.\ 11.44 mean return). This result is consistent with~\cite{b3}: FP3O's
specialized heads each receive only 25\,\% of the fleet's gradient updates with no
compensating specialization benefit in a homogeneous setting.

\textbf{S2 -- Uniform cost multiplier.} Applying a per-type scalar multiplier to all
operations does not alter the ordinal preference ordering; every type still prefers the
cheapest Copy operation. This stage is therefore inconclusive on the specialization
question, as the qualitative optimal policy remains identical across types.

\textbf{S3 -- Operation-specific conflict with confound.} Once operation-specific
multipliers create genuine policy conflict, IPPO continues to dominate FP3O (16.71
vs.\ 8.68 mean return). Investigation of this gap revealed a methodological confound:
each agent's observation included a per-agent one-hot index. Because agent-to-type
assignment was fixed for a given run, IPPO's shared network learned distinct policy
logits conditioned on the agent index, functioning as a per-agent lookup table while
still pooling gradient updates from all four agents. IPPO thus benefited simultaneously
from the sample efficiency of shared weights and the representational capacity of
per-agent specialization. This advantage arose from observation design rather than from
the shared-parameter architecture itself, and directly confirms the caution in~\cite{b14}.

\textbf{S4 -- Corrected re-comparison (5 seeds).} We replaced the per-agent index
with a four-dimensional semantic \texttt{ecu\_type} vector (engine, braking,
infotainment, generic as one-hot), restoring the correct architectural distinction
(Section~\ref{sec:type-cond}). Table~\ref{tab:leaderboard} reports the authoritative
5-seed results. Type-conditioned IPPO and MAPPO are statistically indistinguishable and
nearly deterministic across seeds (CI half-width $\approx 0.001$), showing that full
parameter sharing is sufficient even under genuine ECU-type conflict.

FP3O's headline mean (5.09) is pulled down by a single collapsed seed returning $-32.37$;
the four remaining seeds averaged 14.46, within the IPPO/MAPPO range (Fig.~\ref{fig:comparison}).
FP3O is therefore not systematically weaker in mean performance, but it is substantially
less reliable across random initializations. This instability is visible in the training
curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}), where FP3O's VecNormalize-scaled returns exhibit
persistent high variance while IPPO and MAPPO converge smoothly. The policy entropy
decay curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:entropy}) reinforce this picture: all algorithms transition
from high-entropy exploration toward exploitation, but FP3O's inter-seed entropy band
is substantially wider, indicating that some specialized heads find sharp optima while
others do not. The payload comparison (Fig.~\ref{fig:payload}) shows that FP3O also
transmits approximately 18\,\% more data per episode (45,245 vs.\ 38,335 bytes),
with all three algorithms remaining well above the 5,000-byte deployment target.

Welch's $t$-tests (unequal variance) computed from the per-seed return arrays yield:
IPPO vs.\ FP3O: $t(4.00)=1.23$, $p=0.285$, Cohen's $d=0.78$; IPPO vs.\ MAPPO:
$t(7.99)=2.01$, $p=0.079$, $d=1.27$ (the large effect size reflects near-zero
within-group variance rather than a substantial mean gap). These comparisons are
directionally consistent but not statistically confirmatory at $n=5$. All three
algorithms hold the fleet memory-safety violation rate at \textbf{0.0\,\%} throughout.

\subsection{Interpretation of Type Conditioning}
The S3-to-S4 transition changes the interpretation of the comparison. In S3, the
one-hot agent index is correlated with the fixed ECU-type assignment. A shared
policy can therefore produce different outputs for different physical agents
without changing its parameters. This retains the experience-pooling advantage of
parameter sharing, but it also gives the policy an opportunity to memorize physical
identity. S4 removes this identity information and supplies the semantic ECU-type
vector instead.

The type-conditioned policy can therefore specialize by functional role while
continuing to share all policy parameters and experience. The present result supports
the narrower claim that explicit per-type policy heads are not necessary for the
tested four-agent heterogeneous OTA environment. It does not establish universal
superiority of IPPO, nor does it establish transfer to larger fleets or unseen type
compositions. Those are separate empirical questions.

A useful interpretation is that FP3O implements specialization through parameters,
whereas type-conditioned IPPO implements specialization through input context. The
current experiments show that, under the studied cost conflict, the latter can be
sufficient. Determining the boundary at which increasing heterogeneity makes explicit
specialization preferable is a natural follow-up study.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

The staged experimental design separates the confound-driven advantage from the
genuine architectural comparison. Once the confound is removed, type-conditioned
parameter sharing matches or exceeds FP3O's mean return while offering substantially
lower cross-seed variance. For a fleet-management system selecting an OTA scheduling
policy, this difference in variance carries practical weight: a type-conditioned shared
network is cheaper to train and certify (one set of weights rather than four specialized
heads), and it is less sensitive to random initialization. We recommend that any
MARL-based vehicular scheduler reporting a parameter-sharing result audit its
observation space for per-agent identity leakage of the kind documented in Stage S3.

\textbf{Limitations.} None of the pairwise comparisons reach $p < 0.05$ at $n=5$;
results are directionally consistent but not statistically confirmatory, and scaling
to ten seeds is a direct next step. The root cause of FP3O's collapsed seed has not
been diagnosed: it may reflect a bad random initialization, or a structural
optimization pathology where one specialized head receives too few gradient updates
to escape a poor local minimum. Mean payload cost for all algorithms remains
$7$--$9\times$ above the 5,000-byte deployment target, indicating that further reward
or cost-shaping work is needed regardless of architecture choice. Finally, all
experiments use a synthetic cost model rather than real vehicle telemetry or firmware
images, which limits the external validity of the specific numerical results, though
not of the confound or instability findings themselves.

\section{Conclusion}
We investigated whether a safety-constrained automotive OTA update scheduler requires
explicit per-ECU-type policy specialization. A staged experimental design first
uncovered an agent-identity observation confound that had inflated the apparent
advantage of shared policies, then established a fair comparison under genuine
ECU-type conflict. Across 5 seeds, type-conditioned IPPO and MAPPO matched or exceeded
FP3O's mean return (16.71 vs.\ 5.09) while exhibiting far lower cross-seed variance
(95\,\% CI $\pm0.001$ vs.\ $\pm26.14$). The safety shield held memory violation rates
at 0\,\% throughout for all algorithms. Future work will scale to 10 seeds, diagnose
FP3O's seed collapse, reduce payload cost toward the deployment target, and evaluate
the approach on real vehicle telemetry and larger, more diverse ECU fleets.
