\begin{abstract}
Connected vehicles must keep heterogeneous Electronic Control Units (ECUs) current
via Over-the-Air (OTA) firmware updates. Coordinating updates across engine,
braking, and infotainment ECUs under a \emph{shared} wireless gateway with limited
downlink bandwidth, a hard memory-safety budget, and stochastic channel conditions
is a challenging multi-agent scheduling problem. We formulate this as cooperative
multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) in which each ECU is an independent agent,
supervised by a Control-Barrier-Function (CBF)-inspired safety shield~\cite{ames2019cbf}
and a Monte Carlo Shapley~\cite{shapley1953} credit-assignment scheme. Our central
question is: does coordinating heterogeneous ECU agents under coupled-channel
contention require explicit per-role policy specialization, as FP3O~\cite{b2}
provides, or does naive parameter sharing suffice?

Our authoritative 5-seed, 100,353-timestep coupled-channel benchmark reveals an
empirical pattern that we term \emph{Representation Interference}: in the shared-actor
baselines (IPPO and MAPPO), injecting agent-type identity is associated with lower
return under coupled contention (IPPO: $-27.4$\,pts; MAPPO: $-52.2$\,pts). We interpret
this pattern as evidence consistent with interference between role-dependent learning
signals, rather than as a direct measurement of gradient conflict. FP3O shows the
opposite response, with a $\mathbf{+111.44}$-point improvement ($-213.64 \to -102.20$)
upon type conditioning. IPPO Blind attains the highest mean return in the 100k benchmark,
but its return is statistically indistinguishable from FP3O Type-Conditioned ($p>0.05$)
while its safety-shield intervention rate is substantially higher ($32.66\%$ versus
$13.82\%$). We therefore treat FP3O Type-Conditioned as the preferred operating point
when both task return and reliance on the external safety mechanism are considered.
Long-horizon coupled-channel runs at 2,000,897 timesteps show reward saturation near
$16.71$ for all six configurations, so the 100k results are interpreted primarily as
evidence about early training dynamics and safety behavior rather than final attainable
return.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%%==========================================================================

Modern Software-Defined Vehicles (SDVs) host dozens of heterogeneous ECUs---engine
control modules, anti-lock braking units, infotainment systems---each requiring periodic
firmware refreshes delivered Over-the-Air (OTA) via a shared cellular gateway
\cite{b1,halder2020ota}. Unlike passenger WiFi or navigation map updates, ECU firmware
patches carry strict real-time and memory-budget constraints: a failed or partially
applied update can brick a safety-critical module \cite{b1}. The scheduling problem
is therefore a cyber-physical multi-agent coordination challenge with hard safety
constraints.

A key physical bottleneck is the \emph{shared gateway downlink}: when multiple ECUs
concurrently receive update payloads through the vehicle's cellular connection, they
compete for a common communication resource. Automotive OTA literature identifies
bandwidth, connectivity, and update latency as practical constraints, while cellular
vehicle communication studies commonly use tens of Mbps as a representative high-rate
vehicle link regime~\cite{halder2020ota,scalota,gsma5g}. We therefore set the simulated
gateway capacity to $25$ Mbps as a conservative scenario parameter rather than claiming
that every production TCU operates at exactly this rate. This value is below the
$50$ Mbps experienced downlink reported for urban and high-speed-vehicle scenarios in
5G planning guidance~\cite{gsma5g}, leaving headroom for concurrent vehicle traffic
and protocol overhead. Under this shared cap, simultaneous firmware transfers compete
for the same gateway resource, which creates a meaningful scheduling problem: policies
can reduce contention by temporally staggering transmissions.

Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) is a natural framework for this problem.
The seminal ``surprising effectiveness'' of parameter-sharing PPO \cite{b3} has encouraged
practitioners to train a single shared actor across all agents, leveraging pooled
experience for sample efficiency. FP3O \cite{b2} extends this with per-ECU-type
specialized actor heads on a shared backbone, anticipating that heterogeneous
hardware roles may require structurally disentangled policies.

But which approach is appropriate under bandwidth-coupled, heterogeneous OTA scheduling?
The answer is not determined by parameter sharing alone. We conduct a 5-seed empirical
investigation of a $2\times3$ architecture-by-conditioning design and examine both
return and safety-shield intervention. We use the term \textbf{Representation Interference}
to describe the observed degradation of shared actors after type conditioning under
coupled contention. The mechanism is motivated by the gradient-conflict literature,
but gradients are not directly measured in this study.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A safety-constrained, Shapley-credited multi-agent OTA scheduling environment
    with \emph{coupled gateway downlink contention}, extending~\cite{b1} to the
    cooperative MARL setting with physical bandwidth-coupling physics.
  \item Empirical characterization of a \emph{Representation Interference} pattern:
    shared actor baselines degrade after type conditioning under coupled contention,
    whereas FP3O improves substantially. The observed pattern is discussed in relation
    to gradient conflict, without claiming direct gradient-level verification.
  \item Empirical demonstration that FP3O Type-Conditioned provides the strongest
    combined operating point in the 100k benchmark, with a $+111.44$-point improvement
    over FP3O Blind and a $57.7\%$ lower shield intervention rate than IPPO Blind.
  \item Identification and correction of an agent-identity observation confound that
    silently converts nominally shared policies into per-agent lookup tables,
    confirming the caution in~\cite{b14} with a concrete numerical instance.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%%==========================================================================

\textbf{Vehicular OTA scheduling.}
ReLES-OTA~\cite{b1} formulates single-vehicle OTA scheduling as a codec-construction
MDP solved with single-agent PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo}, reducing payload and memory
overhead versus heuristic baselines. \cite{halder2020ota} surveys electric-vehicle
firmware distribution architectures, highlighting the bandwidth and memory constraints
our simulator models. We extend ReLES-OTA to multi-agent, adding bandwidth-coupled
gateway contention.

\textbf{Cooperative MARL and parameter sharing.}
Centralized-training-with-decentralized-execution (CTDE) has become a standard design
for cooperative MARL. MADDPG~\cite{b10} uses a centralized critic with decentralized
actors, while COMA~\cite{b11} introduces counterfactual credit assignment. Value-based
methods such as VDN~\cite{b12} and QMIX~\cite{b13} factorize team value functions.
For on-policy methods, Yu et al.~\cite{b3} showed that carefully configured IPPO and
MAPPO can match or exceed more elaborate baselines on a range of cooperative tasks,
providing strong evidence for parameter sharing and simple actor-critic designs.

Parameter sharing is attractive because experience collected by multiple agents updates
the same policy representation. Terry et al.~\cite{b14}, however, showed that the
benefit depends strongly on how agent identity is represented and how heterogeneous
observation and action spaces are handled. More recent heterogeneous-agent methods
such as HATRPO and HAPPO explicitly account for heterogeneous policies and sequential
policy updates~\cite{harl}. Other work has explored structured parameter sharing for
physically heterogeneous agents, including unified action-space approaches~\cite{uas}
and heterogeneous skill representations~\cite{hskill}. These studies motivate a
middle ground between one completely shared policy and fully independent networks.
FP3O~\cite{b2} follows this design principle by sharing a representation backbone
while using specialized policy heads for different agent types.

\textbf{Heterogeneity and representation structure.}
The literature suggests that heterogeneity can be handled through agent indicators,
structured action spaces, sequential updates, or explicit role-specific representations.
Our study focuses on a narrower question: when agents have different operation-cost
preferences and compete for a common gateway resource, does adding semantic type
information to a shared actor improve coordination, or can it make optimization harder?
The coupled OTA environment provides a controlled setting in which the answer can be
examined using the same PPO objective across shared and specialized architectures.

The observed degradation after type conditioning is consistent with the broader idea of
conflicting learning signals in shared representations. PCGrad~\cite{yu2020pcgrad}
formalizes gradient conflict in multi-task learning, where task gradients can point in
opposing directions. We use this literature as an interpretation framework rather than
as direct evidence of measured gradient cancellation, since our experiments record
policy outcomes rather than per-role gradient cosine similarities.

\textbf{Vehicular OTA scheduling and bandwidth constraints.}
ReLES-OTA~\cite{b1} formulates single-vehicle OTA scheduling as a codec-construction
MDP solved with PPO, reducing payload and memory overhead relative to heuristic
baselines. Broader automotive OTA studies emphasize connectivity, bandwidth, resource
constraints, security, and update reliability~\cite{halder2020ota,ota_survey}. ScalOTA
also identifies the vehicle-side cellular path as a bandwidth and latency bottleneck
and proposes an alternative distribution architecture~\cite{scalota}. Our work retains
the single-vehicle OTA scheduling setting but introduces multiple heterogeneous ECUs
that share one gateway resource, turning bandwidth contention into part of the learned
coordination problem.

\textbf{Safe MARL and credit assignment.}
Shielding methods constrain RL actions during exploration or execution and can be used
as a safety layer around a learned policy~\cite{alshiekh2018shielding}. Control Barrier
Functions provide another formal framework for maintaining safety constraints in
continuous and cyber-physical systems~\cite{ames2019cbf}. Recent safe MARL work also
studies scalable constrained policy optimization under joint safety constraints~\cite{scalmappol}. Our shield is deliberately simpler: it is a deterministic runtime
filter for the simulated memory constraint. Consequently, shield activation rate is
reported as a behavioral diagnostic rather than as a claim of formal vehicle-level
safety certification. We use Monte Carlo Shapley approximation~\cite{shapley1953,castro2009shapley}
for cooperative credit assignment when heterogeneous operation costs make equal reward
splitting less informative.

\textbf{Training stability.}
We employ Proximal Policy Optimization~\cite{schulman2017ppo} with KL early stopping,
following the trust-region motivation of TRPO~\cite{schulman2015trpo}, and PopArt-style
value normalization~\cite{vanhasselt2016popart}. Invalid-action masking~\cite{huang2020masking}
removes infeasible block selections before sampling. These components are kept identical
across the compared architectures as far as the implementation permits, so that the
main experimental contrast remains architecture and type conditioning.

\section{Environment and Methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Dynamic Data Generation}

We formalize the vehicular OTA firmware update process across heterogeneous ECUs as a
Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP) defined by the
tuple $\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{S}, \{\mathcal{A}_i\}, \mathcal{P}, \{R_i\}, \{\Omega_i\}, \mathcal{O}, \gamma \rangle$.
At time step $t$, the true global state $s^t \in \mathcal{S}$ is not fully visible to
individual ECUs; each ECU agent $i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}$ receives a partial observation
$o_i^t = \mathcal{O}(s^t, i) \in \Omega_i$ and selects an action
$a_i^t \in \mathcal{A}_i$ consisting of a firmware-block and operation pair. The joint
action $\mathbf{a}^t = (a_1^t, \ldots, a_N^t)$ determines the environmental state transition
$s^{t+1} \sim \mathcal{P}(\cdot \mid s^t, \mathbf{a}^t)$ and yields a cooperative team
reward $r_t = R(s^t, \mathbf{a}^t)$ distributed via Shapley coalition values
(Section~\ref{sec:reward}). All policies optimize the expected discounted return:
\[
  J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right], \quad \gamma=0.99.
\]

\textbf{The Environment as a Dynamic Data Engine.}
Unlike classical supervised machine learning regimes that train on static, pre-annotated
corpora or tabular datasets (e.g., Hugging Face, Kaggle, or ImageNet), cooperative
reinforcement learning operates in a closed loop where the \textit{environment itself
replaces the dataset} \cite{sutton2018rl}. In supervised learning, models minimize empirical
risk over a stationary distribution $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_k, y_k)\}$; in our Dec-POMDP, the
data distribution is non-stationary and policy-dependent, actively generated online as
trajectories $\tau = (s^0, \mathbf{a}^0, r_0, \ldots, s^T)$ induced by the evolving policy
$\pi_\theta$. An action chosen by an ECU at step $t$ dynamically alters channel queue
backlogs, transmission delays, and safety hazard exposures encountered at step $t+1$.
Because empirical outcomes are tied to simulator mechanics rather than a static benchmark
file, establishing the physical validity, protocol adherence, and authentic foundation of the
environment is paramount.

\subsection{Coupled Channel Physics}
\label{sec:coupled}

In an uncoupled channel (studied in prior work), each agent experiences an independent
wireless link, so simultaneous transmissions do not interfere. In our
\textbf{coupled-channel} formulation, all ECUs share a single vehicle gateway
downlink with capacity $B_{\text{gw}}$ Mbps. We set $B_{\text{gw}}=25$ Mbps as a
conservative simulation parameter within the tens-of-Mbps vehicle connectivity regime,
rather than as a universal TCU specification. In particular, 5G planning guidance reports
an experienced downlink of $50$ Mbps for urban and high-speed-vehicle scenarios~\cite{gsma5g};
using half of that figure provides a deliberately constrained shared-gateway regime while
leaving room for non-OTA traffic and protocol overhead. When $n_{\text{tx}}$ agents
simultaneously push firmware payloads:
\begin{equation}
  B_{\text{eff}} = \frac{B_{\text{gw}}}{\max(n_{\text{tx}},\,1)},
  \label{eq:coupled}
\end{equation}
degrading each agent's achievable throughput proportionally.

\textbf{Copy Exemption.}
Only \texttt{Modify} (op=1) and \texttt{Modify+Backup} (op=2) operations
increment $n_{\text{tx}}$. The \texttt{Copy} operation (op=0) is strictly exempt:
its delta payload is negligible (${\approx}64$ bytes for a control-plane hash
verification), traversing only the local on-board diagnostic bus rather than the
shared wireless downlink. Penalizing Copy for contention would incorrectly discourage
non-transmitting coordination actions.

A normalized gateway load scalar $\ell_t = n_{\text{tx}} / N$ is injected into the
global observation for all agents during coupled-channel training, giving the
centralized critic and FP3O shared backbone explicit contention feedback.

\subsection{Environment Fidelity and Physical Grounding: The Four Pillars}
\label{sec:authenticity}

To ensure that the simulated Dec-POMDP faithfully models real-world automotive cyber-physical
systems and does not optimize against synthetic artifacts, the environment architecture is
anchored upon four rigorous authenticity pillars:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Pillar I: Automotive Protocol and Safety Standards (ISO 26262 \& ISO 14229).}
  Vehicular electronic control units are not homogeneous processing nodes; they are strictly
  segregated by Automotive Safety Integrity Levels (ASIL) under the ISO 26262 functional safety
  standard \cite{iso26262}. Safety-critical units (e.g., Powertrain, Electronic Braking)
  demand deterministic latency bounds, while Infotainment/Telematics operate on best-effort
  schedules. The memory footprints ($s_i^{\text{mem}}$), block partitioning ($16$ discrete blocks),
  and diagnostic flashing procedures are aligned with the Unified Diagnostic Services (UDS)
  standard ISO 14229-1 \cite{iso14229} over CAN FD and 100BASE-T1 automotive Ethernet gateways.

  \item \textbf{Pillar II: Empirical V2X Wireless Telecommunications Constraints (3GPP \& IEEE 802.11p).}
  The gateway throughput cap ($B_{\text{gw}} = 25$ Mbps) is selected as a conservative
  scenario parameter within the experienced-rate regime reported for vehicle connectivity,
  while the coupled-channel mechanism represents shared-resource contention rather than a
  claim about one specific commercial TCU. 5G guidance reports $50$ Mbps experienced
  downlink in urban and high-speed-vehicle scenarios~\cite{gsma5g}, and automotive OTA
  studies identify bandwidth and connectivity as practical update constraints~\cite{halder2020ota,ota_survey}.
  Rather than introducing an arbitrary penalty for simultaneous transmission, the simulator
  models contention through Eq.~\ref{eq:coupled}, where concurrent wireless transfers divide
  the available gateway capacity.

  \item \textbf{Pillar III: Formally Verified Safety Shielding.}
  Autonomous learning agents exploring high-dimensional action spaces inevitably violate temporal
  deadline constraints during initial training iterations. To prevent flashing failures from
  rendering the vehicle inoperable, the environment incorporates an active deterministic safety
  shield grounded in formal runtime verification and Control Barrier Function (CBF) theory for
  cyber-physical MARL \cite{ames2019cbf, alshiekh2018shielding, dalal2018safe}. When remaining
  steps to deadline reach the minimum necessary flashing duration, the shield overrides non-essential
  actions and forces mandatory update execution.

  \item \textbf{Pillar IV: Standardized Open-Benchmark Infrastructure.}
  To eliminate simulator implementation bias and guarantee complete scientific reproducibility,
  the environment is developed on the Farama Foundation's \texttt{PettingZoo} multi-agent
  standard \cite{b9} and vectorized \texttt{Gymnasium} runtime \cite{b8}. All stochasticity—including
  payload variations, channel jitter, and initial firmware states—is strictly governed by seed-controlled
  pseudo-random number generators. This standardized architecture allows the environment to function
  as an authentic, reproducible community benchmark for vehicular fleet orchestration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Observation and Action Space}

Each agent $i$ observes:
\[
  o_i^t = \bigl[\underbrace{\mathbf{m}_i}_{\text{block mask}},\;
          \underbrace{c_i^{\text{enc}},\, c_i^{\text{tx}}}_{\text{cumulative costs}},\;
          \underbrace{s_i^{\text{mem}}}_{\text{memory}},\;
          \underbrace{k_i}_{\text{step}},\;
          \underbrace{\mathbf{e}_i}_{\text{ecu\_type}},\;
          \underbrace{\ell_t}_{\text{gateway load}}\bigr].
\]
$\mathbf{m}_i \in \{0,1\}^{16}$ is the remaining block mask; $\mathbf{e}_i \in
\{0,1\}^4$ is the one-hot ECU-type vector (engine / braking / infotainment / generic).
Invalid block/operation combinations are masked before policy sampling following
\cite{huang2020masking}, eliminating gradient waste on infeasible actions.

\subsection{Reward Design: Shapley-Valued Coalition Credit}
\label{sec:reward}

Heterogeneous per-type operation costs make naive equal credit-splitting unfair.
We assign each agent its exact marginal contribution via the Shapley
value~\cite{shapley1953}:
\begin{equation}
  r_t^i = \phi_i(v) + r_{\text{prog}},
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\text{prog}} = +0.5$ is a dense progress bonus for executing a valid
collision-free action, and the Shapley value $\phi_i$ measures marginal contribution:
\begin{equation}
  \phi_i(v) = \frac{1}{|N|!} \sum_{\pi \in \Pi}
    \left[v\!\left(S_\pi^i \cup \{i\}\right) - v\!\left(S_\pi^i\right)\right].
  \label{eq:shapley}
\end{equation}
For $|N|\le4$, all $|N|!$ permutations are enumerated exactly \cite{castro2009shapley}.

The coalition value function $v(S)$ evaluates the joint cost of agent subset $S$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  v(S) = \sum_{j \in S \setminus \mathcal{S}_{\text{shield}}}
  \bigl[&-\alpha(C_{\text{enc}}^j + C_{\text{tx}}^j)\,m_{\text{type},\text{op}}^j \\
        &-\beta\,\mathrm{Overhead}^j
         + 10.0\cdot\mathbb{I}(\mathrm{Complete}_j)\bigr],
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:coalition}
\end{equation}
where $C_{\text{tx}}^j$ is the transmission cost derived from $B_{\text{eff}}$
(Eq.~\ref{eq:coupled}); $m_{\text{type},\text{op}}^j$ is the role-specific operation
multiplier (Table~\ref{tab:multipliers}); $\alpha = 10^{-3}$, $\beta = 3\times10^{-4}$
prevent reward magnitudes from reaching thousands. If the grand coalition overflows
the memory budget without a shield: $v(S)=-100.0$, episode truncated.

\textbf{Constraint and safety penalties:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Invalid action (duplicate block): $r_t^i = -15.0$
  \item Step limit exceeded: $r_t^i = -15.0$
  \item Shield intervention (action suppressed): $r_t^i = 0.0$
  \item Unshielded buffer overflow: $r_t^i = -20.0$, episode truncated
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Safety Shield}

A rule-based CBF-inspired filter~\cite{ames2019cbf,alshiekh2018shielding} intercepts
every proposed joint action before execution. If the grand-coalition overhead would
push cumulative fleet memory above $85\%$ of budget, agents with the highest overhead
are blocked in greedy-decreasing order until the constraint is satisfied. The shield
guarantees hard memory-budget compliance independent of policy quality during
exploration. Crucially, shield activation rate is also a behavioral diagnostic. We use ``shield-dependent
behavior'' operationally for a policy that obtains competitive task return while frequently
proposing actions that are subsequently suppressed by the external safety filter. The term
``free-rider'' is used only as shorthand for this observable pattern; it does not imply that
the policy intentionally exploits the shield. Lower intervention rates indicate that more of
the constraint is respected by the learned policy before the external filter is applied.

\subsection{Policy Architectures}
\label{sec:archs}

All architectures follow the CTDE paradigm.

\textbf{IPPO}~\cite{b3}: One shared MLP actor trained with independent PPO across all
agents. Parameters $\theta_{\text{actor}}$ are fully shared; all agents contribute to
a single gradient stream.

\textbf{MAPPO}~\cite{b3}: Shared actor with a centralized critic conditioned on the
joint observation, following~\cite{b10}. The centralized critic provides lower-variance
value estimates during training.

\textbf{FP3O}~\cite{b2}: A shared representation backbone (3-layer MLP:
$256\to256\to128$, ReLU activations) paired with per-ECU-type specialized
\texttt{ActionHead} (3 logits over \{Copy, Modify, Modify+Backup\}) and
\texttt{PositionHead} (16 logits over block positions). The parameter partition is:
\[
  \theta = \bigl\{\theta_{\text{backbone}},\;
                  \{\theta_{\text{head}}^k\}_{k \in \mathcal{K}},\;
                  \theta_{\text{critic}}\bigr\},\quad
  \mathcal{K} = \{\text{Eng}, \text{Brk}, \text{Info}, \text{Gen}\}.
\]
At each forward pass, the 128-d backbone latent is dynamically routed to the
dedicated head for agent $i$'s ECU type---isolating gradients across conflicting
roles at the action-distribution level.

\subsection{Optimization Objective}

All algorithms optimize the PPO clipped surrogate loss~\cite{schulman2017ppo}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)
    + c_1 \mathcal{L}^{\text{VF}}(\theta) - c_2 \mathcal{S}[\pi_\theta],
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\!\left[
    \min\!\left(\rho_t\hat{A}_t,\;\mathrm{clip}(\rho_t, 1\!-\!\epsilon, 1\!+\!\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\text{VF}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\!\left[
    \bigl(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat{R}_t^{\text{PopArt}}\bigr)^2\right],
\end{equation}
where $\rho_t = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|s_t)$,
$\hat{A}_t$ is the GAE advantage~\cite{schulman2015trpo} ($\lambda=0.95$),
$\epsilon=0.2$ is the clipping threshold, $c_1=0.5$, $c_2=0.01$.
KL early stopping~\cite{schulman2017ppo} (threshold $\delta_{\text{KL}}=0.2$) halts
the update epoch if the empirical KL divergence exceeds $\delta_{\text{KL}}$, preventing
policy collapse. Critic targets use PopArt normalization~\cite{vanhasselt2016popart}
(EMA running mean/std) to stabilize value regression across heterogeneous reward scales.

\textbf{Representation Interference Formalized.}
In IPPO/MAPPO with a single shared actor, gradients from all ECU types accumulate:
\[
  \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} =
    \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}^k.
\]
When role-specific objectives favor different actions, the corresponding updates to
shared parameters can compete. For example, Engine and Infotainment agents have different
operation-cost preferences in our coupled environment. This creates a plausible route to
shared-representation interference, analogous to gradient conflict in multi-task learning~\cite{yu2020pcgrad}.
We did not directly measure pairwise gradient cosine similarity, so the experiment supports
this mechanism as an interpretation of the behavioral result rather than as a verified
optimization-level measurement. FP3O instead assigns role-specific action and position
heads, reducing direct parameter sharing at the final policy-distribution stage.

\subsection{Type Conditioning vs.\ Agent-Identity Conditioning}
\label{sec:type-cond}

A subtle but critical design decision concerns \emph{what} semantic content is
provided to the shared actor. Early experiments included a per-agent numeric one-hot
index (\texttt{agent\_id}) in the observation. Because ECU-type assignment is fixed
within a run, the agent-index and ECU-type are perfectly correlated. A shared policy
conditioned on \texttt{agent\_id} therefore learns per-agent lookup logits while still
pooling gradient updates---simultaneously benefiting from shared sample efficiency
and per-agent expressivity. This is a form of the observation-design confound
documented in~\cite{b14}: the shared policy is not truly ``shared'' in its
representational capacity.

We corrected this by replacing \texttt{agent\_id} with a four-dimensional one-hot
\texttt{ecu\_type} vector (engine / braking / infotainment / generic). Under this
design, agents of the same ECU type are indistinguishable to the policy, and the
shared policy must genuinely generalize across individuals within each type.

\subsection{Development Lifecycle}
\label{sec:lifecycle}

The research proceeded through eight structured phases; Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}
summarizes the key engineering milestones. Phase~1 replicated the ReLES-OTA
single-agent baseline~\cite{b1}. Phase~2 extended to PettingZoo Parallel
API~\cite{b9} with SuperSuit vectorization~\cite{b9}, death masking, and stochastic
wireless channels. Phase~3 stabilized training via PopArt normalization~\cite{vanhasselt2016popart},
KL early stopping~\cite{schulman2017ppo}, invalid-action penalty tuning ($-1\to-15$),
and step-budget expansion. Phase~4 introduced ECU heterogeneity and uncovered the
\texttt{agent\_id} confound, correcting it with one-hot \texttt{ecu\_type}.
Phase~5 implemented FP3O with dynamic batch routing. Phase~6 generalized network
parameters from region-specific to generic constrained CPS conditions. Phase~7 implemented
coupled-channel contention with Copy exemption. Phase~8 conducted the authoritative

%%==========================================================================
\section{Experimental Setup}
%%==========================================================================

\textbf{Environment.}
$N=4$ agents, $16$ blocks per agent, coupled gateway $B_{\text{gw}}=25$ Mbps,
memory limit $M_{\text{budget}}$, safety-shield threshold $85\%$. Stochastic
channel: truncated Gaussian latency ($\mu=120$ ms, $\sigma=15$ ms), $5\%$ packet
loss, burst jitter.

\textbf{Training.}
$N_{\text{envs}}=4$ parallel environments; rollout buffer $N_{\text{steps}}=256$;
effective buffer size $4\times256=1{,}024$ samples per update. Mini-batch size
$128$ $\Rightarrow$ $8$ gradient steps per epoch (optimizing signal-to-noise
ratio). Adam optimizer~\cite{kingma2015adam}, learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$.
Entropy coefficient $c_2=0.01$; clipping $\epsilon=0.2$; $n_{\text{epochs}}=10$;
KL threshold $\delta_{\text{KL}}=0.2$.

\textbf{Evaluation horizons.}
Two horizons serve complementary roles:
(i) \emph{100,353 timesteps}: the transient sample-efficiency regime where
coordination skills form and policy differences are maximal---our primary benchmark.
(ii) \emph{2,000,897 timesteps}: a long-horizon validation regime used to test whether
the early differences persist. In the coupled-channel runs, all six architecture/conditioning
configurations eventually approach the same empirical reward ceiling near $16.71$.
This long-horizon reward saturation limits the usefulness of final return as a discriminator,
so the 100k benchmark is retained for comparing early learning dynamics and shield behavior.

\textbf{Seeds and statistics.}
$N_{\text{seeds}}=5$ per configuration, following NeurIPS/ICLR MARL benchmarking
conventions~\cite{b3}. Results are reported as mean $\pm$ 95\% CI across seed-level
mean returns. Welch's $t$-tests (unequal variance) assess pairwise significance;
at $n=5$, results are directional unless $p<0.05$.

\textbf{Algorithms.}
All implemented in Stable-Baselines3~\cite{b20} with FP3O policy registered as a
custom SB3 \texttt{ActorCriticPolicy}. IPPO and FP3O share the actor; MAPPO adds a
centralized critic. All trained with the same PPO objective (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}).

\textbf{Baseline hardware.}
CPU-only training (AMD Ryzen 7, 16 GB RAM); one seed at $100$k steps takes
${\approx}3$--$4$ min; all 30 runs (6 configurations $\times$ 5 seeds) completed
in under $3$ hours.

\subsection{Failure of Naive Parameter Sharing under Contention}

In both IPPO and MAPPO, enabling type conditioning lowers the 100k return:
IPPO drops $27.4$ points ($-85.27 \to -112.64$), while MAPPO drops $52.2$ points
($-135.70 \to -187.88$). Shield activation also remains substantial in both baselines.
We use these observations as the empirical signature of the phenomenon termed
\emph{Representation Interference}. The result is consistent with a shared actor
having difficulty accommodating role-dependent learning signals under coupled contention,
but the present study does not directly measure the gradients needed to establish
cancellation at the optimization level.

Under blind conditioning, the actor receives no semantic ECU-type indicator. Its policy
therefore cannot explicitly distinguish the operation-cost preferences encoded by the
four roles. Under type conditioning, the same parameter set must represent role-dependent
responses. The degradation observed for IPPO and MAPPO suggests that the additional
semantic information does not automatically improve coordination when it is introduced
through a single shared policy head.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_interference.pdf}
\caption{Representation Interference summary: $\Delta$Return when switching from
Blind to Type-Conditioned per architecture. Negative bars indicate policy degradation
in the shared-actor baselines; the positive FP3O bar shows the gain obtained when its
specialized heads receive semantic type information.}
\label{fig:interference}
\end{figure}

\subsection{FP3O's Structural Resilience}

FP3O exhibits the opposite response: type conditioning produces a
$\mathbf{+111.44}$-point improvement ($-213.64 \to -102.20$). In blind mode, the
specialized heads do not receive semantic type information, so the architecture cannot
reliably route observations to role-appropriate output behavior. With type conditioning,
each ECU type is routed to its corresponding action and position heads. The result is a
large improvement in the 100k coupled-channel benchmark and supports the architectural
hypothesis that limited role-specific parameterization can be useful when operation costs
and contention patterns differ by ECU type.

\subsection{Autonomous Coordination vs.\ the Free-Rider Shield Phenomenon}

IPPO Blind achieves a higher mean return ($-85.27$) than FP3O Type-Conditioned
($-102.20$) in the 100k benchmark. Their 95\% confidence intervals overlap substantially
($\pm173.39$ versus $\pm113.58$), and the reported Welch test gives $p>0.05$.
The return difference should therefore not be treated as evidence that IPPO Blind is
statistically superior.

The two configurations nevertheless exhibit markedly different shield behavior. IPPO Blind
triggers the external safety filter in $32.66\%$ of steps, compared with $13.82\%$ for
FP3O Type-Conditioned. We describe the former as \emph{shield-dependent} or, more briefly,
``free-riding'' only in this operational sense: the learned policy frequently proposes
actions that the external governor subsequently suppresses while the aggregate return
remains competitive. This terminology does not imply intentional exploitation by the
policy. From an engineering perspective, a policy that requires fewer interventions leaves
more of the safety constraint satisfied by the learned behavior itself.

FP3O Type-Conditioned therefore provides the preferred trade-off in our evaluation. It
does not maximize raw 100k return, but it combines competitive return with the lowest
shield intervention rate among the six configurations in the primary benchmark.

\subsection{Early Training Differences and Long-Horizon Reward Saturation}
\label{sec:asym}

The 100k coupled-channel benchmark is intentionally paired with a longer 2,000,897-step
coupled-channel validation. The purpose of the long run is not to rank final returns, but
to determine whether the early differences remain visible after extended optimization.
Across the six configurations, the return eventually approaches an empirical ceiling near
$16.71$. The convergence is consistent with the structure of the reward: the $+10.0$
completion bonus and the repeated $+0.5$ progress bonuses can dominate the scale of the
episode return once agents reliably complete their backlogs. We therefore refer to this
effect as \textbf{long-horizon reward saturation}. At that point, final return provides
little information about the quality of the coordination policy itself, which motivates
reporting the transient 100k results and shield intervention rate separately.

The long-horizon result changes how the 100k comparison should be interpreted. We do
not claim a fixed $20\times$ sample-complexity advantage, because all six configurations
can eventually reach the same reward ceiling in the extended coupled-channel runs. The
stronger and better-supported conclusion is that FP3O Type-Conditioned reaches a more
favorable early-training operating point, combining substantially lower shield intervention
with a large improvement over FP3O Blind. This distinction matters for applications where
training or policy adaptation must be stopped before reward saturation, although the present
study does not quantify the engineering cost of retraining in a production OTA pipeline.

%%==========================================================================
\section{Discussion and Limitations}
%%==========================================================================

\textbf{Practical implications.}
The 100k benchmark suggests that architecture and conditioning should be evaluated jointly
in bandwidth-coupled OTA scheduling. IPPO Blind obtains the highest mean return, but its
shield intervention rate is substantially higher than that of FP3O Type-Conditioned, while
the two returns are statistically indistinguishable at the reported sample size. Under a
safety-aware evaluation criterion, FP3O Type-Conditioned is therefore the preferred
operating point in our study. The result also reinforces the importance of auditing
observation spaces for agent-identity leakage~\cite{b14}, evaluating policies under
shared-channel contention, and reporting shield intervention alongside mean return.

\textbf{Scalability.}
All experiments use $N=4$ agents and $16$ blocks. Modern vehicles host $30$--$80$
ECUs. At larger $N$, exact Shapley computation (factorial in $N$) becomes infeasible;
we use exact enumeration at $N\le4$ and Monte Carlo sampling~\cite{castro2009shapley}
for larger $N$. FP3O's $O(N_{\mathcal{K}})$ head overhead scales with the number of
ECU types, not the number of individual agents---a favorable property.

\textbf{Confidence and sample size.}
At $n=5$, the wide and overlapping 95\% CIs mean that return differences should be
interpreted cautiously. In particular, the higher mean return of IPPO Blind over FP3O
Type-Conditioned is not statistically distinguishable at the reported sample size. The
shield-rate contrast is nevertheless operationally important because it captures a distinct
aspect of policy behavior, although a dedicated statistical test of shield events would be
needed for stronger inference.

\textbf{Long-horizon reward saturation.}
The $+10.0$ completion bonus and dense progress reward compress the return scale after
extended training. Future experiments should reduce the dominance of these bonuses or
add coordination-sensitive metrics such as contention cost, completion time, and shield
interventions. Such changes would make long-horizon comparisons more informative.

\textbf{Synthetic cost model.}
All results use a simulated OTA cost model rather than real vehicle telemetry or firmware
images, limiting the external validity of the numerical results. The observed architectural
pattern is useful as a hypothesis for real deployments, but its transferability must be
validated with hardware-in-the-loop or vehicle telemetry before operational conclusions are
drawn.


%%==========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%%==========================================================================

We investigated how semantic type conditioning and policy architecture affect
heterogeneous ECU coordination under a shared OTA gateway. In the 100k coupled-channel
benchmark, shared IPPO and MAPPO show lower returns after type conditioning, a behavioral
pattern we interpret as consistent with representation interference. FP3O shows the opposite
trend: type conditioning improves its return by $+111.44$ points and reduces shield
interventions from $64.68\%$ to $13.82\%$ relative to FP3O Blind. IPPO Blind achieves
the highest mean return in this transient benchmark, but its $32.66\%$ shield rate is much
higher than FP3O Type-Conditioned, and the return difference is not statistically
significant at $n=5$. For a safety-aware operating criterion, FP3O Type-Conditioned is
therefore the preferred configuration in the studied regime.

The 2,000,897-step coupled-channel runs add an important qualification: all six
configurations eventually approach an empirical return ceiling near $16.71$. This
long-horizon reward saturation means that final return alone cannot distinguish the learned
coordination strategies. The main evidence of architectural difference therefore lies in
the transient learning regime and in the degree to which policies rely on the external
shield. Future work should use coordination-sensitive long-horizon metrics, scale beyond
four ECUs, evaluate additional seeds and heterogeneous team compositions, and validate the
coupled-channel model with real vehicle or hardware-in-the-loop measurements.
